Product Strategy Case Study

Chegg Ledger

AI knows whats public. Chegg nows what actually happened. A pitch for turning fifteen years of student data into something professors can't get anywhere else.

CompanyChegg, Inc.
RoleProduct Strategy
BuyerProfessors and departments
Executive Summary

Stop competing where AI already won

Chegg's core business is answers. Free AI tools give students answers faster and better now. That fight is already lost, and no version of smarter homework help wins it back.

Chegg Ledger sells something else. It's a fifteen year record of where real students, at real schools, get stuck on a given course or textbook. That record is proprietary, it predates generative AI, and it can't be scraped or reconstructed. The buyer is the professor, not the student. Someone who wants to know a course's rough spots before teaching it.

Problem

This isn't a product problem

Chegg's subscriber losses track closely with ChatGPT's rise. Step by step answers used to be worth paying for, now they're free and instant, from a dozen different tools. 

Any strategy that keeps Chegg in the business of giving answers, even AI powered ones, is fighting on extensive ground in a growing industry. The only path left is finding what Chegg has that AI doesn't.

Approach

What made the cut?

Three earlier versions of this pitch got cut once they were tested against a hard question.

Cut

AI flags AI solvable assignments

Had Chegg's AI scan a syllabus and flag which questions students could just ask ChatGPT. A professor can ask ChatGPT the same question directly. AI wrapped around Chegg's data isn't a moat, it's a feature anyone rebuilds in a weekend.

Cut

A student facing companion tool

An app showing students their own weak spots, sold alongside the professor product. Dropped so the pitch has one buyer instead of two, and one reason to pay.

Cut

"Students are training this"

Early language implied ongoing participation. That's not accurate. No student opts into anything. The record is a byproduct of them solving their own problem, already logged, going back fifteen years.

Why This Holds Up

Two questions worth asking first

Isn't this what Canvas or Quizlet already do? No. Those tools only see one professor's own class, after the fact, on questions that professor chose to test. A new professor or a first time course starts with nothing. Chegg already has the pattern: how students taking this exact textbook chapter at hundreds of other schools, over many years, have struggled, including the parts nobody put on a quiz. 

Doesn't AI already train on years of internet history too? A different kind of history. A model learns from public writing about a topic: forum posts, old homework help sites, textbook excerpts. It can tell you a topic confuses people in general. It has no access to Chegg's actual usage logs: which student searched which exact question, in which exact course, and how often. That data was never posted publicly. It lives inside Chegg's own systems. No model has seen it and none can rebuild it from what's on the open web.

  • On moats

    Adding AI to a pitch feels like the safe move. The data underneath is usually the actual moat, and AI wrapped around it just invites comparison to whatever AI tool the buyer already has.

  • On scope

    Removing the student facing half kept the pitch focused on one buyer and one reason to pay.

  • On wording

    "Historical usage record" and "students are training this" describe the same data. Only one is accurate, and only one holds up under a hard question.
